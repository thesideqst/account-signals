Revenue jumped to $96.2 billion, more than double what NVIDIA pulled in a year ago. That headline number sits at the heart of a quarter where every line on the income statement swelled, yet the profit story tells a subtler tale.

Cost of revenue rose to $24.1 billion, up 17.7 percent quarter‑over‑quarter and 86.8 percent year‑over‑year. Gross profit climbed to $72.1 billion, a 18.0 percent rise quarter‑over‑quarter and 113.1 percent year‑over‑year. Operating income hit $63.7 billion, up 19.0 percent quarter‑over‑quarter and 124.1 percent year‑over‑year. Net income reached $59.7 billion, a modest 2.3 percent increase quarter‑over‑quarter but a 125.9 percent jump year‑over‑year.

The numbers paint a picture of explosive top‑line growth, but the bottom line’s modest quarter‑over‑quarter lift hints at something else. That contrast is where management’s narrative and the raw data begin to diverge.

Toshiya Hari opened the call by reminding listeners that the discussion would be “based on information currently available” and that forward‑looking statements carried risk. He set the stage for the prepared remarks that followed.

Colette Kress took the reins and framed the quarter as “another outstanding” one, emphasizing that total revenue “more than doubled year‑over‑year.” She highlighted a “fourth consecutive quarter” of accelerating growth, and she projected a roughly 70 percent revenue increase in fiscal 2028, calling the outlook “supply‑constrained.” The focus on supply constraints was meant to temper expectations, yet the balance sheet showed inventory swelling to $32 billion as the company readied for the Vera Rubin launch. The inventory rise is a concrete sign that the supply bottleneck is already being stocked, even as management warns that supply will remain a limiting factor through fiscal 2028.

Kress detailed the data‑center split. She said data‑center revenue rose 18 percent quarter‑over‑quarter to $89 billion, with hyperscale revenue at $49 billion and ACIE revenue at $40 billion. The ACIE segment, which includes NeoCloud, industrial and enterprise customers, grew 25 percent sequentially and 138 percent year‑over‑year. She touted a “global infrastructure build‑out” backed by a $2 trillion cloud industry backlog and capex by the top five hyperscalers projected at $800 billion in 2026 and $1.3 trillion in 2027. Those figures underscore the scale of demand that is fueling the revenue surge.

When Kress turned to the product roadmap, she announced an expansion of the partnership with AWS, adding 2 million GPUs through fiscal 2029, and she described the upcoming Vera Rubin platform as delivering “30 times higher throughput per megawatt” and “35 times lower token costs” versus the previous Grace Blackwell Ultra. She positioned Vera Rubin as the fastest product ramp in NVIDIA’s history, implying that the next wave of revenue will be driven by this new generation.

The CFO also highlighted cash returns. NVIDIA gave back $26 billion to shareholders, split between $20 billion in share repurchases and $6 billion in dividends. That payout represented 60 percent of free cash flow on a year‑to‑date basis, exceeding the company’s own target of returning at least half of free cash flow.

Margins, however, introduced a note of caution. GAAP and non‑GAAP gross margins held at 75 percent, unchanged from the prior quarter. Kress warned that memory price pressure would push Q3 gross margins down to 74 percent, with a further dip to the low 70s in Q4 before settling in the low 70s for fiscal 2028. The memory scarcity she described is a direct result of the same AI demand that is inflating revenue, creating a feedback loop that could compress profitability even as sales climb.

The Q&A session let Jensen Huang’s voice rise above the prepared script. He emphasized that AI agents now consume “15 to 100 times” more compute than a human would, and that the company is the only one offering a full‑stack AI factory platform. Huang pushed back on the notion that supply constraints could be ignored, noting that “we have supply for 70 percent” of demand and that the rest of the supply chain—land, power, cooling, labor—must be aligned to keep up. He framed the supply challenge as a coordinated effort across memory suppliers, power generators, and land‑power‑shell partners.

Huang also underscored the breadth of NVIDIA’s addressable market. He said the “other half” of AI compute, the ACIE segment, is “invisible to everybody” because those customers don’t buy chips one at a time; they need an entire factory platform. He pointed to NeoClouds in Armenia, Africa, Taiwan, India, Australia, Malaysia, and elsewhere, each pairing local land and power with NVIDIA’s stack. The narrative here is that growth is not just a hyperscaler story; it’s a global, multi‑segment expansion that management believes will double year‑over‑year.

When pressed about margins, Huang reminded listeners that each gigawatt of data‑center capacity now yields roughly $40 billion of revenue opportunity, up from $18 billion five years ago. He argued that this productivity translates into “insanely profitable” token generation, reinforcing why customers are racing to the latest generation—first Hopper, then Grace Blackwell, now Vera Rubin.

The tension between the numbers and the narrative sharpens around profitability. Net income’s 2.3 percent quarter‑over‑quarter rise is dwarfed by the 17.9 percent revenue jump. Operating income grew 19 percent, but the modest net income lift suggests higher expenses or tax impacts. Kress did note that GAAP and non‑GAAP operating expenses rose 10 percent and 11 percent sequentially, driven by “high compute infrastructure costs and compensation and benefits.” She also mentioned a non‑GAAP effective tax rate of 16 percent, up from the prior year because of higher revenue. The data points to a company that is spending aggressively to capture market share, even as it returns cash to shareholders.

Another divergence appears in the outlook. Kress projected third‑quarter revenue of $108 billion, plus or minus 2 percent, and a fiscal 2028 revenue growth of roughly 70 percent. Yet she warned that supply will stay a bottleneck through the end of fiscal 2028. Huang echoed the supply constraint but added that the company “has supply for 70 percent” of demand, implying a gap that could widen if demand accelerates faster than the supply chain can respond.

The call closed with a reminder that Jensen will appear at upcoming conferences and that the next earnings call is slated for November 17. Those dates mark the next checkpoints for the story that is still unfolding.

So where does the uncertainty lie? The quarter delivered record revenue and a flood of new partnerships, but the modest net‑income growth, rising inventory, and explicit warnings about memory scarcity and supply constraints leave a question unanswered: can NVIDIA’s supply chain expand fast enough to keep pace with the AI compute demand that is now the engine of both revenue and profit? The answer will shape the next chapter of this AI‑driven surge.
