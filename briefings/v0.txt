Revenue hit $96.221 billion. That’s up 17.9 percent quarter over quarter and up 105.9 percent year over year. Cost of revenue rose to $24.079 billion, a 17.7 percent sequential gain and an 86.8 percent jump from a year ago. Gross profit climbed to $72.142 billion, up 18.0 percent quarter over quarter and 113.1 percent year over year. Operating income reached $63.734 billion, a 19.0 percent rise from the prior quarter and a 124.1 percent increase from the same quarter last year. Net income was $59.688 billion, up 2.3 percent sequentially and up 125.9 percent year over year. All of these figures are derived from the SEC XBRL filings, not directly reported line items.

Colette Kress opened the prepared remarks by calling the quarter “another outstanding quarter with record revenue, operating income and EPS.” Her language matches the headline numbers. She said total revenue more than doubled year‑over‑year, which aligns with the 105.9 percent growth we just heard. She also highlighted an 18 percent quarter‑over‑quarter rise in data‑center revenue to $89 billion. That figure sits inside the broader $96 billion total and reinforces the same upward trend the GAAP numbers show.

When Kress turned to the data‑center split, she said hyperscale revenue hit $49 billion and grew 13 percent sequentially. The overall data‑center revenue growth of 18 percent that she cited is consistent with the $24 billion rise in cost of revenue, which is driven largely by data‑center spend. Her claim that “more compute drives more revenue as new GPU capacity comes online” is reflected in the 19 percent jump in operating income, showing that higher spend is translating into higher profitability.

Kress also emphasized ACIE revenue of $40 billion, up 25 percent sequentially and 138 percent year over year. That segment’s surge helps explain why net income rose only modestly—2.3 percent quarter over quarter—despite the massive top‑line growth. The ACIE boost lifts gross profit, but the modest net‑income increase signals higher operating costs, which she later confirmed by noting a 10 percent rise in GAAP operating expenses and an 11 percent rise in non‑GAAP operating expenses.

The CFO’s commentary on margins is a key point of divergence. GAAP and non‑GAAP gross margins held at 75 percent this quarter, unchanged from the prior period. Kress warned that memory pricing pressure will push Q3 gross margins down to 74 percent, with a dip to the low 70s in Q4 before settling in the low 70s for fiscal ’28. The numbers we have today do not yet show that dip, but the guidance signals a future gap between the current 75 percent level and the expected 71‑72 percent range. That is a material shift to watch.

Kress also highlighted a supply‑constrained outlook, projecting roughly 70 percent revenue growth in fiscal ’28. The guidance for Q3 revenue of $108 billion, plus or minus 2 percent, is consistent with the 17.9 percent sequential growth we just saw. Her supply‑constraint language matches the fact that inventory rose to $32 billion as the company prepared for the Vera Rubin launch. The inventory increase underscores the constraint she described.

On the cash side, Kress said the company returned a record $26 billion to shareholders, with $20 billion in share repurchases and $6 billion in dividends. That aligns with the strong free‑cash‑flow generation implied by the $59.688 billion net income and the 60 percent return of free cash flow year‑to‑date, which exceeds the 50 percent target she referenced.

The CEO, Jensen Huang, used the Q&A to paint a broader picture. He said AI agents consume 15 to 100 times more compute than a human using the same tool. That anecdote explains why the data‑center spend is exploding, but it also underscores why the company’s margins may feel pressure from memory scarcity. He reiterated that NVIDIA is the only company offering a full AI‑factory platform, a point that dovetails with Kress’s claim that the “full‑stack AI factory” is a unique capability driving growth.

Huang also stressed the importance of both closed and open models. He listed a roster of closed models—OpenAI, Anthropic, Groq, Meta, Gemini—and open models—TML, Mistral, Qwen, Kimi, GLM, DeepSeek, MiniMax, Nemotron. That breadth supports Kress’s statement that NVIDIA “runs every model.” The numbers back that up: the data‑center revenue surge and the ACIE growth both stem from customers running a wide variety of workloads on the same platform.

In the Q&A, Huang noted that each gigawatt of data‑center capacity now supports roughly $40 billion of revenue opportunity, up from $18 billion five years ago. That metric ties directly to the $89 billion data‑center revenue figure Kress quoted. It also explains the operating‑income rise to $63.734 billion, because higher revenue per gigawatt translates into higher operating leverage.

Huang highlighted the partnership with AWS, which will deploy an additional 2 million GPUs through Q2 fiscal ’29. That future supply will help close the gap between the 70 percent growth target and the current supply constraint. It also reinforces the “Vera Rubin accounting for about 20 percent of data‑center revenue in Q3” guidance Kress gave. The AWS commitment is a concrete example of the supply pipeline she described.

When pressed on margins, Huang acknowledged memory price spikes and said the company is resetting expectations. He echoed Kress’s forecast of 74 percent gross margin for Q3 and a dip to the low 70s in Q4. The alignment between management’s forward‑looking statements and the current 75 percent margin gives the executive a clear narrative: margins are strong now, but a modest decline is expected as memory costs rise.

The Q&A also revealed a strategic focus on the “ACIE” half of the business—so‑called AI‑native, sovereign, and enterprise customers. Huang said that half of NVIDIA’s data‑center revenue now comes from this segment, growing at 100 percent year over year. That matches Kress’s claim that ACIE grew 138 percent year over year to $40 billion. The numbers confirm that the “non‑hyperscaler” side of the business is no longer a niche; it is a major growth engine.

Huang warned that the company shipped less than 1 percent of its total data‑center revenue in Hopper 200 products to China, reflecting U.S. licensing restrictions. That detail explains why the company’s forward outlook excludes China data‑center revenue. It also underscores the importance of the supply‑chain diversification Kress mentioned, including the new Vera CPU and Vera Rubin GPU lines.

Both speakers stressed the importance of the CUDA ecosystem. Kress said the ecosystem “extends AI into markets a single chip alone can never reach.” Huang added that open models run on NVIDIA because the CUDA stack is everywhere—from PCs to edge devices to data centers. The combination of a broad software stack and a diversified hardware portfolio is reflected in the 75 percent gross margin and the strong operating‑income growth.

In summary, the headline numbers tell a story of explosive top‑line growth, solid gross margins, and rising operating income. Management’s language largely mirrors those facts. Where they diverge is in the forward‑looking margin outlook, where memory pricing will shave a point or two off gross margins. They also differ in emphasis: Kress focuses on supply constraints and the ACIE opportunity, while Huang emphasizes the breadth of model support and the strategic importance of partnerships like AWS and the Frontier AI labs. The executive can repeat that revenue is $96.221 billion, up 105.9 percent year over year, with gross profit at $72.142 billion and operating income at $63.734 billion. The company is returning $26 billion to shareholders and expects Q3 revenue of $108 billion, plus or minus 2 percent. Margins are currently 75 percent but are projected to dip to the low 70s later this year due to memory scarcity. Supply is constrained at about 70 percent of demand, but new partnerships and the Vera Rubin rollout aim to close that gap. The ACIE segment is now roughly half of data‑center revenue and is growing at double‑digit rates, providing a diversification beyond hyperscalers. All of these points give the account executive a concise, data‑driven narrative to use in meetings with customers and internal stakeholders.
