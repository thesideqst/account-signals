The data center revenue hit $89 billion, a record that smashed every forecast and left the supply chain scrambling. That jump pushed total revenue to $96.221 billion, more than double what the company reported a year ago. Cost of revenue climbed to $24.079 billion, up 17.7 percent quarter‑over‑quarter and 86.8 percent year‑over‑year. Gross profit rose to $72.142 billion, up 18.0 percent quarter‑over‑quarter and 113.1 percent year‑over‑year. Operating income reached $63.734 billion, up 19.0 percent quarter‑over‑quarter and 124.1 percent year‑over‑year. Net income hit $59.688 billion, up 2.3 percent quarter‑over‑quarter and 125.9 percent year‑over‑year. The numbers tell a story of explosive growth, but they also hint at pressure points that management chose to gloss over.

[Toshiya Hari, prepared_remarks] “Good afternoon, and welcome to NVIDIA’s conference call for the second quarter of fiscal 2027.” The opening set the stage for a celebration. [Colette Kress, prepared_remarks] “We delivered another outstanding quarter with record revenue, operating income and EPS.” She followed with a headline: “Total revenue of $96 billion more than doubled year‑over‑year as growth accelerated for the fourth consecutive quarter.” The emphasis was on the double‑digit surge, not on the rising cost base.

Colette then turned to the engine of that surge. “Q2 data center revenue increased 18 percent quarter‑over‑quarter to $89 billion.” She highlighted hyperscale and ACIE contributions, noting $49 billion from hyperscale and $40 billion from ACIE. “Hyperscale revenue grew 13 percent sequentially, driven by sustained strength in Blackwell.” She added that the cloud industry backlog now exceeds $2 trillion and that the top five hyperscalers will spend $800 billion in 2026 and $1.3 trillion in 2027. The narrative painted a picture of limitless demand.

The next point was supply. “We expect to grow revenue by approximately 70 percent in fiscal 2028. This is a supply‑constrained outlook.” The company framed the future as a shortage of chips, not a lack of customers. Colette announced an expansion with AWS, “deploying an additional 2 million GPUs starting this quarter through the second quarter of fiscal ’29.” She also described the NeoCloud rollout, “expected to exit the year with 8 gigawatts in total installed capacity, up from approximately 3 gigawatts at the end of 2025.” The message was clear: demand is outpacing the ability to ship.

When the CFO turned to margins, the tone softened. “GAAP and non‑GAAP gross margins were both 75 percent, largely unchanged from last quarter.” She noted operating expenses up 10 percent quarter‑over‑quarter, driven by compute infrastructure and compensation. Inventory rose to $32 billion, a sign that the company is loading up for the Vera Rubin launch. Days sales outstanding stretched to 60 days, reflecting extended payment terms for large purchases.

The outlook section sounded optimistic but also cautious. “Total revenue is expected to be $108 billion, plus or minus 2 percent, for the third quarter.” Colette warned that “GAAP and non‑GAAP gross margins will be 74 percent plus or minus 50 basis points for Q3, then bottom in Q4 at 71 percent to 72 percent before settling at 72 percent to 73 percent in fiscal ’28.” The margin guidance acknowledged the pressure from memory scarcity that she described as “extreme pricing conditions in memory.”

The prepared remarks painted a picture of unstoppable growth, a supply bottleneck, and a modest margin dip. The raw numbers, however, reveal a different texture. Cost of revenue surged 86.8 percent year‑over‑year, a rise that dwarfs the 17.9 percent revenue increase. Inventory jumped to $32 billion, a level not mentioned in the upbeat narrative. The 2.3 percent quarter‑over‑quarter net‑income rise is modest compared with the 19 percent operating‑income jump, suggesting that higher costs are already eating into profitability. Management’s focus on “record revenue” and “70 percent growth” sidesteps the fact that the cost structure is inflating faster than sales.

The Q&A session let the executives speak without a script. [Jen‑Hsun Huang, qa] opened with a reminder that “AI agents use an enormous amount of compute, 15 to 100 times more than a human using them.” He stressed that “the part that people don’t see about our growth is that we are practically singular because of the nature of how we deliver products.” He described the supply chain as a “land, power, shell” problem that takes two to three years to secure. “We have supply for 70 percent, we have more supply than 70 percent, but about 70 percent,” he said, underscoring the gap between demand and capacity.

Huang also highlighted the full‑stack AI factory. “We created the world’s first rack‑scale architecture with NVLink 72. We are now in our third generation.” He explained that each gigawatt of data‑center capacity now represents $40 billion of revenue opportunity, up from $18 billion five years ago. The story reinforced the idea that “the productivity is X‑factor increase in each generation” and that “customers want to race to the next generation as fast as they can.” The emphasis on productivity and revenue per gigawatt aligns with the company’s claim that “Vera Rubin accounts for about 20 percent of data‑center revenue in Q3.”

When pressed about margins, Huang acknowledged memory scarcity. “Memory scarcity today is driven in large part by the AI build‑out itself.” He framed the higher component costs as a symptom of demand, not a purely negative factor. The tone was defensive: “We are working with all three major memory suppliers to increase capacity.” The narrative tried to turn a cost pressure into a sign of market strength.

The CFO, Colette, returned to the balance sheet. “We returned a record $26 billion to shareholders, $20 billion through share repurchases and $6 billion through our quarterly dividend of $0.25 per share.” She noted that the company is returning 60 percent of free cash flow year‑to‑date, exceeding its 50 percent target. The cash‑return story masks the fact that the same quarter saw inventory rise sharply and days sales outstanding stretch, hinting at cash being tied up in the supply chain.

Analysts asked about the “unconstrained” growth. Huang answered that the unconstrained growth was “100 percent year‑over‑year this year,” but that “we have to work hard to get more capacity.” He reiterated that “the other half of the picture is ACIE – enterprise, NeoClouds, sovereign AI – that represents about half of our business and is growing 100 percent a year.” The emphasis on the “invisible” half of the market underscores why the company can claim massive growth while the headline numbers already reflect a doubling.

The tension between the numbers and the narrative crystallizes around three themes. First, revenue and operating income are soaring, but cost of revenue and inventory are rising faster, eroding margins. Second, management paints supply constraints as a temporary bottleneck, yet the balance sheet shows a deliberate buildup of inventory and extended payment terms, suggesting that the bottleneck may be structural. Third, the forward‑looking growth target of 70 percent in fiscal 2028 sits on a foundation of a 105.9 percent year‑over‑year revenue jump, but the margin guidance predicts a dip to the low 70s percent range, a shift not fully explained in the prepared remarks.

What remains unresolved is how the company will reconcile the aggressive growth outlook with the mounting cost pressures and the supply‑chain constraints that already appear in the numbers. The answer will determine whether the next quarter’s revenue surge translates into sustainable profitability or simply fuels a cycle of higher spending and thinner margins.
