The headline this quarter? NVIDIA shipped more than ninety‑nine gigawatts of AI compute and still fell short of the demand it just created.  

Revenue jumped to ninety‑six point two two one billion dollars, up seventeen point nine percent quarter‑over‑quarter and up one hundred five point nine percent year‑over‑year.  Gross profit rose to seventy‑two point one four two billion dollars, up eighteen point zero percent quarter‑over‑quarter and up one hundred thirteen point one percent year‑over‑year.  Operating income climbed to sixty‑three point seven three four billion dollars, up nineteen point zero percent quarter‑over‑quarter and up one hundred twenty‑four point one percent year‑over‑year.  Net income hit fifty‑nine point six eight eight billion dollars, up two point three percent quarter‑over‑quarter and up one hundred twenty‑five point nine percent year‑over‑year.  Cost of revenue sat at twenty‑four point zero seven nine billion dollars, up seventeen point seven percent quarter‑over‑quarter and up eighty‑six point eight percent year‑over‑year.  

Those numbers tell a story of explosive growth across the board, but the narrative the executives chose to tell is a different one.  Colette Kress opened the call by calling the quarter “record” and by pointing to a “more than doubled” revenue year‑over‑year.  She highlighted a “fourth consecutive quarter” of accelerating AI demand and a “supply‑constrained outlook.”  The CFO then projected a seventy percent revenue increase in fiscal twenty‑twenty‑eight, insisting that the company’s growth would be limited by supply, not demand.  

The tension shows up when you compare the raw growth rates with the language of constraint.  Revenue grew a little under twenty percent quarter‑over‑quarter, yet the company is already talking about being unable to meet demand.  Operating income rose nineteen percent quarter‑over‑quarter, but Kress warned that gross margins would slip to seventy‑four percent in the next quarter because memory prices were “extreme.”  The CFO said GAAP and non‑GAAP operating expenses were up ten percent and eleven percent respectively, and then added that operating expenses would stay in the low‑fifties percent range for the full year as the product portfolio broadens.  

Management’s focus on the supply side is reinforced by a parade of partnership announcements.  Kress announced an expansion with Amazon Web Services that will see an additional two million GPUs deployed through the second quarter of fiscal twenty‑nine.  She said AWS will run the Nemotron family of open models on Bedrock and SageMaker and will adopt the full AI stack—Omniverse, Cosmos, Isaac and Jetson—to power warehouse robots.  The same day, she detailed a rollout of the Vera Rubin platform, describing it as delivering “thirty‑times higher throughput per megawatt” and “thirty‑five times lower token cost” than the previous generation.  She called Vera Rubin “the fastest product ramp in NVIDIA’s history.”  

Yet the numbers on the balance sheet hint at a different pressure point.  Inventory swelled to thirty‑two billion dollars, a clear sign that the company is building stock to meet anticipated demand.  Days sales outstanding stretched to sixty days, reflecting longer payment terms for large, multi‑quarter purchases.  The company returned a record twenty‑six billion dollars to shareholders, but that cash outflow also reduces the buffer needed to fund the next wave of capacity.  

When analysts pressed on the supply bottleneck, Jensen Huang answered with a broader view of the AI ecosystem.  He reminded listeners that “AI agents use an enormous amount of compute” and that the compute required for an agent can be fifteen to one hundred times what a human would need.  He described NVIDIA’s platform as “the only company in the world that creates and builds an entire AI factory platform.”  Huang emphasized that the company’s advantage lies in its ability to run “every model, every workload, from the edge to the cloud.”  He also warned that “memory scarcity today is a symptom of the same demand surge that’s driving our own growth.”  

The contrast between the two voices is stark.  Kress paints a picture of a company that is deliberately throttling its own shipments to preserve pricing power, while Huang frames the same constraints as an industry‑wide shortage that only a few players can navigate.  Both agree that the next generation of hardware—Vera Rubin, the Vera CPU, and the Groq 3 LPX accelerator—will be the engine of future growth, but they differ on how quickly that engine can be turned on.  

One concrete metric underscores the supply tension.  The CFO said the company expects Vera Rubin to account for about twenty percent of data‑center revenue in the third quarter, yet the same quarter’s revenue guidance is a narrow band of plus or minus two percent around one hundred eight billion dollars.  If Vera Rubin delivers on that share, the remaining eighty percent must still come from existing products that are already facing memory‑price pressure.  The CFO also warned that GAAP and non‑GAAP gross margins will dip to seventy‑four percent in the third quarter, then bottom out in the low seventies in the fourth quarter before settling in the low seventies for fiscal twenty‑twenty‑eight.  

The earnings call also revealed a strategic shift in how NVIDIA monetizes its infrastructure.  Kress described a “revenue‑sharing structure” with NeoCloud partners that gives lenders a minimum revenue guarantee while allowing NVIDIA to earn a share of any upside.  She called this model “the potential to drive billions in revenue over the medium to long term.”  The same structure appears in the company’s financing of Frontier AI labs, where NVIDIA has pledged nearly fifty billion dollars in capital and partnered with six major infrastructure investors to raise half a trillion dollars of third‑party capital.  Those moves suggest that NVIDIA is not just selling chips; it is building a financial ecosystem around compute.  

All of this comes against a backdrop of an unprecedented AI spend.  Global venture capital funding for AI reached four hundred billion dollars in the first half of twenty‑twenty‑six, dwarfing the two hundred sixty‑five billion dollars raised in all of twenty‑twenty‑five.  Nearly twenty AI‑native companies now exceed a one‑billion‑dollar annualized run‑rate, up from thirteen a quarter ago.  In the enterprise segment, on‑prem revenue in automotive hit eight billion dollars, while financial services, manufacturing and healthcare together contributed seven billion dollars.  These figures illustrate that the market outside the hyperscalers is expanding at a pace that could rival the traditional cloud segment.  

Yet the call also exposed a blind spot.  The CFO noted that less than one percent of data‑center revenue in the quarter came from Hopper 200 products shipped to China, reflecting U.S. licensing restrictions.  He said there is “no China data‑center compute revenue in our forward outlook.”  The implication is that a sizable portion of the global demand curve is being carved out of the company’s addressable market, a factor that does not appear in the growth guidance.  

What does all of this mean for the next quarter?  The company will try to push more Vera Rubin units into the market while wrestling with memory‑price volatility and a supply chain that is still catching up to demand.  The CFO expects operating expenses of roughly nine billion dollars for the quarter, and the CFO reiterated a tax rate between sixteen and eighteen percent for the full year.  Meanwhile, Huang kept the focus on the broader AI lifecycle, insisting that the “four phases” of data preparation, pre‑training, post‑training and agentic inference all rely on NVIDIA’s architecture.  He warned that “the world will need both closed models and open models,” and that NVIDIA’s CUDA ecosystem is the only platform that runs every frontier model.  

The story ends with a question that no one on the call answered directly:  As AI demand continues to outpace the ability to produce silicon, memory, power and land, will NVIDIA’s supply‑constrained outlook become a self‑fulfilling prophecy, or can the company’s new financing structures and product‑ramp speed close the gap fast enough to keep the growth engine humming?
